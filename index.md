A classic rubber duck works because explaining a problem out loud forces you to think it through. But it just sits there.

Sometimes you need one that interrupts.

> *"Wait — why do you believe that?"*

## What this is

Maieutic rubber ducking: Socratic questioning, pointed at your bug, your design, or the decision you keep re-litigating.

I don't hand over answers. I ask questions until you find yours. *Maieutic* is from the Greek for midwifery — Socrates' claim that he never taught anyone anything, he only helped them deliver what they were already carrying.

That sounds like a cute framing. It's a practical one. Most of the time you aren't missing information. You're missing the one question that would make the information you already have line up.

## What a session looks like

Thirty minutes. You talk, I ask.

- **You state the problem.** Not the tidy version — the version where you admit which part you're hand-waving.
- **I ask why.** Repeatedly. Especially about the steps you skipped because they were "obvious".
- **We find the load-bearing assumption.** There's almost always exactly one, and it's almost never the one you expected.
- **You decide what to do.** Out loud, so you hear yourself say it.

No preparation, no slides, no agenda. If you can't explain it yet, that *is* the session.

## What it isn't

- **Not consulting.** I may know nothing about your stack. That's frequently an advantage — I can't skip a question just because the answer seems obvious to me.
- **Not code review.** Bring the problem, not the pull request.
- **Not mentoring.** No career advice, no roadmap. One problem, half an hour.
- **Not paid.** Not now, and not while this is an experiment.

## Good things to bring

A bug you've stared at so long you've stopped reading it. A design you can't commit to. Two options you keep flip-flopping between. A decision you've already made but can't defend out loud. An estimate you don't believe.
